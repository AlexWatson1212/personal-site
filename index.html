Where a design comes from

What understanding a practice actually changes.

For a client, the website is the second thing that happens. Here is that order traced through one of the three: a single observation about the people who write to Maya Bennett, and what it went on to settle.

  1. What was understood

    People arrive mid-thought. Most are not in crisis, most have never done this before, and they open by apologising — for not having a proper reason, for taking a place somebody else might need more.

  2. What that decided

    Then the site cannot ask for a prepared account before it will accept contact. Whatever version somebody has now has to be enough to begin with.

  3. What the site says

    “Most people start in the middle of a sentence.”

    The largest type on the first screen — the reader’s state, not a service and not a promise.
  4. What the site does

    The enquiry asks for a name, an email address and a format, with “not sure yet” already selected. The message is optional, and beside it one line: “I’d like to talk to someone” is a complete enquiry. Everything practical — the fee, the availability, the cancellation charge, the two reduced-fee places — is published on the page, so nobody has to make contact in order to ask.

One thing understood about who is reading, and it has settled the headline, the practical section and the enquiry form. None of that is a visual preference. Sofia Marin and Daniel Mercer settle the same three things from different observations about different readers — which is why the three sites read nothing alike.

Every line above is either quoted from the published Maya Bennett document or checkable on the site it describes. Maya Bennett is a fictional practice designed and built by the studio; its document was written after the concept site, in the form a client’s is written before one.

Why three, and not one house style

Two of the three, side by side.

Maya Bennett and Daniel Mercer are both one integrative counsellor, working with adults, at the same fee for the same fifty minutes. Both are about the moment before somebody makes contact. Nothing on the two pages agrees about anything — because the practices behind them do not, and the design is downstream of that.

Case 02Maya Bennett

Case 03Daniel Mercer

  1. PaletteMaya is warm paper and plum, with apricot kept for one thing only: the moment something is met. Daniel is black on off-white with one electric blue that appears only on things you can press. One accent each, doing opposite jobs.
  2. TypographyMaya uses a soft serif for what a visitor brings and a plain grotesque for what is said back, set at the same size as the sentence it answers. Daniel has no serif anywhere: a very large grotesque headline that behaves like a statement in a room, and facts set as facts.
  3. StructureMaya demonstrates rather than asserts: a sentence people say, and the reply to it, already on the first screen. Daniel answers a fear with a fact in every section and puts the first step within reach of each.
  4. WordsDaniel removes an entry requirement: you do not have to be good at talking. Maya argues the requirement was pointing the wrong way: the unprepared account is the more useful place to start. Neither promises an outcome.

Both are fictional practices designed and built by Alexander Watson Studio as portfolio work. Neither is a commissioned client project, and no counselling service is offered by either.
