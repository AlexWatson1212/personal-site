Where a design comes from

What understanding a practice actually changes.

The website is the second thing that happens. Here is the first, on one of the six: a single observation about the people who write to Maya Bennett, and what it went on to settle.

  1. What was understood

    People arrive mid-thought. Most are not in crisis, most have never done this before, and they open by apologising — for not having a proper reason, for taking a place somebody else might need more.

  2. What that decided

    Then the site cannot ask for a prepared account before it will accept contact. Whatever version somebody has now has to be enough to begin with.

  3. What the site says

    “Most people start in the middle of a sentence.”

    The largest type on the first screen — the reader’s state, not a service and not a promise.
  4. What the site does

    The enquiry asks for a name, an email address and a format, with “not sure yet” already selected. The message is optional, and beside it one line: “I’d like to talk to someone” is a complete enquiry. Everything practical — the fee, the availability, the cancellation charge, the two reduced-fee places — is published on the page, so nobody has to make contact in order to ask.

One thing understood about who is reading, and it has settled the headline, the practical section and the enquiry form. None of that is a visual preference, and none of it survives being applied to a different practice — which is why the other five sites do none of it.

Every line above is either quoted from the published Maya Bennett document or checkable on the site it produced. Maya Bennett is a fictional practice designed and built by the studio.

Why six, and not one house style

Two of the six, side by side.

Daniel Mercer and Stillpoint are both one counsellor, working with adults, at a similar fee, in the same year. Nothing on the two pages agrees about anything — because the practices behind them do not, and the design is downstream of that.

Case 03Daniel Mercer

Case 06Stillpoint

  1. PaletteDaniel is black on off-white with one electric blue that appears only on things you can press. Stillpoint is stone, fog and paper with a single rust accent reserved for links. Both use colour as instruction rather than mood, and arrive at opposite places.
  2. TypographyDaniel sets a very large grotesque headline that behaves like a statement in a room. Stillpoint runs one serif at one weight and lets scale alone carry emphasis, because its practitioner refuses to stage his own authority.
  3. StructureDaniel answers a fear with a fact in every section and puts the first step within reach of each. Stillpoint gives whole bands to a single sentence, and keeps the four practical facts in the footer of every page instead.
  4. WordsDaniel removes an entry requirement: you do not have to be good at talking. Stillpoint removes a different one: you do not have to become a person with a diagnosis and a vocabulary. Neither promises an outcome.

Both are fictional practices designed and built by Alexander Watson Studio as portfolio work. Neither is a commissioned client project, and no counselling service is offered by either.
