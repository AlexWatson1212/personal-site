There isn’t a threshold

Counselling for the part before you’ve decided it counts.

There isn’t a threshold you have to cross first. People often arrive apologising — for taking a space someone else might need more, for not having a proper reason, for being upset about something they’ve already decided is small. None of that is asked for here.

Counselling isn’t only for the worst fortnight of your life. It’s also for the long, functional stretch where everything looks fine from the outside and you’ve quietly stopped feeling much about any of it.

What brings people

You might recognise one of these.

None of them need tidying up before you get here. Each one below is something somebody has actually said, and each one has an answer beside it.

“I’m fine. I’ve been fine for about four years.”

I’d ask what fine has involved. That’s an hour, easily.

“I say yes, then spend three days resenting it.”

We could start with the last time that happened. Nothing bigger needed.

“I got the thing I wanted and felt almost nothing.”

That’s worth an hour. You wouldn’t have to explain the nothing first.

“I can’t tell if I’m unhappy or just tired.”

You don’t have to decide which before we speak.

“Everyone else seems to have a next step.”

We can start there. It doesn’t need a bigger reason behind it.

“I keep having the same argument with different people.”

Bring the most recent one. The details do most of the work.

“It ended months ago and I still can’t explain why.”

Not being able to explain it isn’t a problem for a first session.

“I know what I should want. That isn’t the same thing.”

That’s a sentence I’d want to sit with. It’s enough to book a call on.

Before a first session

You don’t need to prepare.

People often think they should arrive with a tidy account — background in order, when it started, the actual problem, and what they want from therapy. Almost nobody has that, including the people who look like they do.

The untidy version is more useful anyway. It’s closer to how you actually think about it, and it hasn’t had the interesting parts smoothed out of it yet.

What people think they need to bring

  • The background, in order
  • When it started
  • The actual problem, stated clearly
  • What I want from therapy

What is genuinely enough

  • Something that happened on Tuesday
  • A feeling that hasn’t got a story yet
  • The thing I’ve never said out loud
  • An argument I keep replaying
  • “No idea, honestly”

Both are a first session. Only one of them takes six weeks to write.

What sessions are like

Fifty minutes a week with someone who isn’t in a hurry to fix you.

Same time each week, in an ordinary room with good light and a door that shuts. No couch, no clipboard, no technique performed at you.

You can be funny about it. Most people are, at first.

The shape of it

You talk. I listen properly, which is less passive than it sounds. I’ll ask questions, and some of them you won’t have an answer to — that tends to be the interesting part. Nothing is compulsory: “I don’t want to go into that today” is a complete sentence.

Integrative, meaning

I don’t run one method over everything regardless of fit. I’ve trained across several approaches and choose between them depending on what’s in front of us — sometimes that means understanding where a pattern began, sometimes it means what to do about Thursday.

Relational, meaning

What happens between us counts. How it feels to talk to me, what comes easily and what you find yourself editing — that isn’t the preamble to the work, it’s a fair amount of the work. Most of us learned how to be with people somewhere, and it turns up in the room.

Maya

I’m not a blank screen.

I trained in Bristol and have been in independent practice for five years. Before counselling I worked in arts programming, which mostly taught me how much of what someone means arrives in the pause before they say it.

In the room I’m fairly informal and I use plain words. You’ll usually be able to tell when something lands with me. What I won’t do is tell you how to live, or agree with everything automatically — I’d rather say what I notice, including when it’s inconvenient. That’s the part people say they didn’t expect to like.

I work with adults on their own, mostly in their twenties, thirties and early forties, and often with people who have never done this before and are faintly embarrassed to have booked.

How to start

Three steps, and you can stop after any of them.

  1. Send a message

    A few lines is plenty. You don’t have to explain the whole situation or find the right words for it.

  2. A short call

    Twenty minutes, free, usually within a few days. You can ask me anything — including what I’m like to talk to.

  3. A first session

    If it seems workable, we book one. Afterwards you decide whether to carry on, and stopping later takes one sentence. There’s no block of six to commit to.
