# Showing the proposition to therapists — the questions, and how to use the answers

Alexander Watson Studio · internal · written 10 September 2026

**Not published.** `docs` is in the `exclude` list in `_config.yml`. These
questions are for conversations with therapists, supervisors and counselling
peers. **They must not appear as a section on the public website.** A site that
asks its visitors to critique its own offer is asking them to do the studio's
work, and it undermines the confidence the rest of the site is built on.

---

## The six questions

Ask them in this order. They move from what is working, through what is unclear,
to what would stop somebody paying — which is the order in which people are
willing to say difficult things.

1. **What feels genuinely valuable here?**
2. **What is unclear?**
3. **What would make you hesitate before paying for this?**
4. **What would you worry might go wrong?**
5. **Is there anything you expected to be included that isn't?**
6. **If you were starting private practice, what would make this substantially
   easier for you?**

---

## The principle

**Listen rather than defend.**

The instinct when somebody misreads the offer is to explain it better in the
room. That converts a piece of evidence into a conversation and destroys it: if
the page needed explaining, the page is the problem, and the explanation you just
gave is the fix. So take the answer, write it down, and resist the correction.

**A repeated objection is evidence. A single preference is not.**

If three people out of eight stumble at the same sentence, that sentence is
wrong. If one person would prefer a different colour, a call instead of a form,
or a payment plan, that is one person's preference and changing the product for
it is how a coherent offer turns into a shapeless one. The threshold is
repetition, not intensity — the most forcefully expressed objection is not
therefore the most common one.

**Distinguish three kinds of answer**, because they need three different
responses:

| Kind | Example | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The offer is misunderstood | "So the Practice Clarity part costs extra?" | Fix the words. This is a site problem. |
| The offer is understood and unwanted | "I want to be able to edit it myself." | Change nothing. Say so honestly and early — the FAQ already does. |
| The offer is understood and something real is missing | "There's nothing about what happens if I go on maternity leave mid-project." | Consider it. Write it down first, decide later, not in the room. |

## How to record it

One file per conversation, in `_strategy/` (never published), named
`feedback-YYYY-MM-DD-<who>.md`. Their words rather than a summary of them,
because the phrasing is the data — "I don't know what I'd actually get" and "I
don't know if it's worth it" are different objections with different fixes.

After each conversation, add one line to a running tally of which of the six
questions produced something the site could act on. After eight or so
conversations, the tally is the argument for what to change.

---

## Questions deliberately not on the list

- *"Would you buy this?"* — People say yes to be kind and no to be safe, and
  neither answer predicts anything. Question 3 gets the useful half of it.
- *"What would you pay for this?"* — Asks somebody to price work they have not
  had. It produces a number that feels like data and is not.
- *"Do you like the design?"* — The site's whole argument is that a design is
  downstream of a practice. Asking this invites the aesthetic conversation the
  work is trying to replace.
