## The short answer

There is no standard price. A therapist in the UK can have a website for
the cost of a monthly subscription they set up themselves, or for a
project fee that runs into thousands of pounds, and both can be the right
decision for a particular practice.

What you are really paying for is not the website. It is how much of the
thinking, writing, design and upkeep somebody else does for you, and what
you are left holding when they have finished.

## Why prices vary so much

Three things account for most of the difference.

**Who does the thinking.** A template gives you a layout and leaves the
hard part — deciding what to say, and in what order — to you. At the other
end, somebody works out how the practice should be described before
anything is designed. That work takes time, and it is usually the part
that makes the difference to who gets in touch.

**Who writes the words.** Many projects are priced on the assumption that
you supply the copy. Writing it for you, from what you have said about
your practice, is a significant part of the work, and a common reason one
quote is much higher than another.

**How much is being built.** A single well-made page is a different job
from a ten-page site with a blog, online booking and a newsletter. More
pages mean more writing, more design decisions and more to maintain.

## The common ways therapist websites are priced

**A website builder you set up yourself.** You pay a monthly or annual
subscription and do the rest. It is the cheapest route in money and the
most expensive in your time, and the result is only as clear as what you
put into it.

**A website that comes with a directory listing or a platform.** Some
directories and practice-management services include a simple site with
membership. It is convenient; the page usually looks like everyone
else's, and it tends to disappear if you stop paying.

**A fixed-price project with a freelancer or small studio.** You pay once
for an agreed scope. What that scope contains varies widely, which is
why the questions at the end of this note matter more than the headline
figure.

**An agency project.** More people, more process and usually more pages.
Right for a larger organisation; often more than a single private
practice needs.

**A monthly "website as a service".** A lower upfront cost, then a
monthly fee for as long as you have the site. Worth checking whether you
own anything at the end, and what happens to the site if you stop.

## What tends to increase the cost

- Several pages rather than one
- Copy written for you rather than supplied by you
- A custom identity — a logo or wordmark, colours and typography — rather
  than a template's defaults
- Online booking, payments, a blog or other integrations
- Photography
- Ongoing changes after launch, if they are not included

## When a cheaper option is the sensible one

If you already know exactly what you want to say, are comfortable writing
it, and are happy to spend an afternoon or two setting it up, a builder
subscription can be a perfectly good answer. If your
[directory profile](/guidance/counselling-directory-profile/) is already
bringing the enquiries you want, you may not need a website yet at all.

Spending more does not make a practice clearer. It only helps if the money
buys work you would not otherwise get done.

## What "cheap" can leave out

The gaps are usually not visible on launch day. They show up later:

- The domain registered in somebody else's name, so you cannot move
- No way of getting your site out if you stop paying
- Nobody to fix it when something technical breaks
- Words that describe any therapist, not you
- Every small change after launch charged separately, or not possible at all

None of these is a reason to avoid a cheaper option. They are reasons to
ask about them before you choose.

## What a therapist actually needs

Less than most websites contain. Someone arriving on your site wants to
know whether you work with people like them, what it is actually like,
what it costs, that you are a real person, and how to get in touch.
[What belongs on a therapist's homepage](/guidance/homepage-contents/)
goes through those five in order.

For many private practices that fits on one carefully made page. More
pages are worth paying for when you have more to say, not because a
website is expected to have them.

## Questions to ask before you choose someone

1. Is the domain registered in my name, in my own account?
2. Do I own the finished website, and can I take it elsewhere?
3. Who writes the words, and from what?
4. What exactly is included, and what would cost extra?
5. How many rounds of feedback are there, and what counts as one?
6. What does a change cost after launch?
7. What happens if I stop paying for hosting or care?
8. Is the price written down before I commit?

Whoever you choose, a clear answer to each of those is worth more than a
lower headline figure.
