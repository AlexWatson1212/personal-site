Short answer: you need one photograph of yourself. Whether you need
anything else depends on what your practice is, and for most solo
practitioners the answer is no.

## The one that matters

A person deciding whether to contact a therapist is doing something
slightly frightening. A photograph of the actual human being lowers that
by more than any sentence on the page.

It needs to be:

- **Recent.** Recognisably you, now.
- **Well lit.** A window on an overcast day is better than most indoor
  lighting and better than direct sun.
- **Looking at the camera.** Not gazing thoughtfully into the middle
  distance.
- **Reasonably plain.** A background that is not busy. Clothes you would
  actually wear to work.

That is the whole specification. A friend with a recent phone and twenty
minutes will produce something usable. A professional will produce
something better, and if you can afford it and it makes you less
self-conscious about having your photograph taken, it is money well
spent — but it is not the difference between a website that works and
one that does not.

## What a photographer is genuinely worth paying for

**If you are a practice rather than a person.** Several practitioners,
photographed consistently, is a job. Mixed phone photographs of six
people in six different lights look worse than no photographs at all.

**If your room is part of the offer.** A dedicated space you want people
to see before they arrive — particularly for work with children, families
or anyone for whom the environment matters — is worth photographing
properly.

**If you have a specific reason to look a particular way.** Working with
organisations, doing supervision or training, presenting at conferences:
those audiences read photography differently from a person looking for
counselling.

**If having your photograph taken is difficult for you.** This is the
underrated one. A good photographer's actual skill is making somebody
comfortable enough to look like themselves. If you have been avoiding
the whole subject for two years, that is what you are paying to solve.

## What you almost certainly do not need

Stock photography. The category has a fixed vocabulary — hands around a
mug, pebbles balanced on a beach, a path through a misty wood, an empty
armchair, somebody looking out of a rain-streaked window — and a
prospective client has seen all of it on four other websites this
evening. It does not read as calm. It reads as unspecific.

If a page needs something in that space, better options are: nothing at
all, a photograph of the actual room, or a considered piece of colour and
shape that belongs to the design rather than pretending to be a
photograph of a feeling.

## A note on the room

If you photograph your consulting room, photograph it as it is, with the
light it actually has. A room that has obviously been staged is worse
than no room, because the first thing it tells somebody is that they are
being sold to.

Do not photograph anything that could identify a client, and do not use
photographs of people who are not you unless they have agreed to it in
writing and know where it will appear.

## The honest summary

One good photograph of you. The room, if the room matters. Nothing else,
unless there is a specific reason.

Most therapy websites would be improved more by deleting three stock
images than by adding a photoshoot.
