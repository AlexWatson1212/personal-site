There are two versions of the therapist About page.

The first is four lines long. Qualifications, membership body, a
sentence about being committed to providing a safe and confidential
space. It is correct and it is completely anonymous.

The second is a thousand words about a personal turning point.

Both are written by people who find the task uncomfortable, which is
most people. The first solves the discomfort by disappearing. The second
solves it by over-explaining.

## What the page is actually for

A prospective client is not reading your About page to learn about you.
They are reading it to work out what it would be like to be in a room
with you, and whether they would be able to say the thing they have not
said out loud yet.

That reframes the question. It is not *how much should I disclose*. It
is *what does this person need in order to imagine the first session*.

## What usually helps

**Something specific about the work.** Not "I work with anxiety" but the
shape of it — what you notice, what you tend to be interested in, what
you think tends to be underneath the thing people arrive with. Two or
three sentences of real thought does more than a paragraph of adjectives.

**A sense of how you are.** Warm, direct, quiet, dry, structured. You do
not have to name it. It comes through in how the page is written, which
is why an About page written in your own words beats one written to
sound professional.

**Something plainly human.** Where you live. What you did before this, if
you did something before this. A sentence that could only have been
written by you. It does not need to be a revelation — the point is that
somebody wrote it.

**The practical facts.** Training, accreditation, supervision, insurance,
how long you have been practising. Stated once, without ceremony.

## What usually does not

**A therapeutic autobiography.** Your own history of therapy, your
recovery, your reason for entering the profession. Sometimes this is
exactly right — practitioners working in a specific field often need to
say it. But it is worth being honest about who it is for. If it is
mainly there to justify your presence in the room, the page will feel
like it is asking the reader for something.

**A modality lecture.** A prospective client does not know what
integrative means, and explaining it well takes more words than the page
can spare. Better: describe what you do, and name the modality once for
the people who are looking for it.

**Everything.** The About page does not have to carry your whole
professional identity. It has to get somebody to the point of sending an
email.

## The line, roughly

You are aiming for the amount of self-disclosure you would offer a new
colleague at the end of a good first conversation. More than a CV. Less
than a confidence.

## A practical way to write it

Ask somebody who knows you to describe what you are like to talk to,
and write the page from what they say rather than from what you think
you should sound like. Most people's own drafts are more formal, more
distant and less likeable than the way they actually are.

Then read it aloud. If a sentence would be embarrassing to say to
somebody sitting opposite you, it is probably a sentence written for an
imagined professional audience rather than for a client.

## And the photograph

Have one. It does more work than the whole page.

It does not need to be expensive, styled or flattering. It needs to look
like you on an ordinary day, taken somewhere with decent light, looking
at the camera. Somebody deciding whether to contact you is trying to
picture a person. Give them one to picture.
