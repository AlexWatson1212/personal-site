Someone has found your website. They may have been given your name, or
they may have clicked three others before yours. Either way they arrived
with a question, and they will decide within about a minute whether you
can answer it.

They are not reading. They are scanning for five things.

## 1. Whether they are in the right place

Not "welcome to my practice". The specific version: who you work with,
and what you work with them on.

> Counselling for adults in Sheffield, mostly around anxiety, burnout
> and work that has stopped feeling like it fits.

That sentence does more than a paragraph about your approach, because it
lets somebody rule themselves in or out immediately. Both outcomes are
useful. The person who leaves was never going to become a client, and
they have now left without occupying an enquiry slot.

If you genuinely work with a broad range, say the broad thing plainly
rather than listing twenty presenting issues. A list of twenty reads as
a directory profile, not a practice.

## 2. What it is actually like

This is the part most websites skip, and the part most people want.

Not your modality. What happens. How long a session is. Whether it is in
a room or on a screen. Whether they can email you first rather than
ringing. Whether you will ask them to fill something in.

A person considering therapy for the first time has no model for any of
this. Two sentences of plain description does more for their nerves than
any amount of language about safe spaces.

## 3. What it costs

Put the fee on the homepage or one clear click from it.

Withholding a price does not create an enquiry. It creates a person who
emails to ask the price, gets an answer, and disappears — or, more often,
one who does not email at all. Fee transparency is being argued for
inside the profession for good reasons, and it also happens to remove the
single most common reason somebody closes a therapist's website.

If you have a sliding scale, say the range and say how it works. If you
have a small number of lower-cost places, say that. Vagueness reads as
expensive.

## 4. Whether you are real

One photograph of you. Not a stock image, not a chair, not a plant.

You do not have to like being photographed. The photograph is not there
to be flattering — it is there so that the person can picture sitting in
a room with a human being rather than with a website. A plain, well-lit,
unstyled photograph is better than an expensive one where you look like
somebody else.

Your registration and membership belong here too, set out exactly as
your professional body permits, without turning the page into a wall of
badges.

## 5. What to do next

One action, said in plain words, repeated where a person is likely to be
ready rather than everywhere.

"Send me an email and tell me a little about what is going on" is a
better call to action than "book now", because it describes something
the person can actually imagine doing. If there is a form, keep it
short. Every field is a small reason to stop.

And say what happens afterwards. *I usually reply within two working
days.* That one sentence removes more anxiety than most of the rest of
the page.

---

## The order

Roughly:

1. Who you work with and on what
2. What it is like, in practical terms
3. Fees
4. Who you are, with a photograph
5. How to get in touch, and what happens then

Everything else — your training history, your reading, your
accreditations in full, your thoughts on the therapeutic relationship —
belongs on an About page or a Fees page, where the person who wants it
will go looking.

## What does not need to be there

A blog you will not maintain. A quote about the journey of a thousand
miles. A list of every modality you have ever encountered. An
introductory video you have been meaning to record for a year. A
newsletter signup. Testimonials, which most professional bodies restrict
anyway.

None of these are wrong in themselves. They are just not what the person
scanning your homepage came for.

## One test

Read your homepage as though you were somebody who has never had
therapy, is slightly embarrassed to be looking, and is doing this at
eleven at night.

If that person can tell within a minute whether you might be the right
person, and knows what to do about it, the page is doing its job.
