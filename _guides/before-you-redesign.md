People usually decide to redesign a website because they have stopped
liking it. That is a legitimate reason, but it is worth ten minutes
working out what is actually wrong first, because the answer changes
what you should do about it.

Roughly, there are three situations.

## Situation one: it works, you are just bored of it

The information is right, the enquiries are the right sort, nothing is
broken. You have simply looked at it four hundred times.

This is real, and it is not nothing — a website you are embarrassed by
is a website you do not send people to. But it does not need rebuilding.
It usually needs a photograph, a rewritten opening paragraph and some
space.

## Situation two: it is out of date rather than badly designed

Your fees changed. You stopped taking new referrals for something. You
moved. You added online sessions and never updated the page. The About
page still describes a practice you had four years ago.

Fixing this takes an afternoon and no money. Do it before you decide
anything else, because a redesign that carries over stale content just
produces a nicer-looking version of the same problem.

## Situation three: the website cannot do what you need it to

The enquiries are the wrong sort, or there are not enough of them, or
you cannot bring yourself to give people the address. Something more
structural is off — usually who the site is talking to, what it explains,
or how easy it is to take the next step.

That is the situation a new website solves.

---

## The checklist

Work through these honestly. Where an answer is no, note whether it is a
content problem or a design problem.

**1. Does the first screen say who you work with and on what?**
Not "welcome". The specific thing.

**2. Is your fee findable in under ten seconds?**
If not, that is likely costing you more enquiries than the design is.

**3. Is there a recent photograph of you?**
One. Looking at the camera.

**4. Would a person who is not right for you be able to tell quickly?**
If everybody feels invited, you will keep receiving enquiries you have to
turn down.

**5. Does it work properly on a phone?**
Open it on your own phone, at arm's length, in a room with the lights
on. Most of your visitors are doing exactly that.

**6. Can somebody contact you without ringing?**
A great many people considering therapy will not make a phone call to a
stranger. If email or a form is not offered plainly, you are filtering
for confidence rather than fit.

**7. Do you say what happens after they contact you?**
"I usually reply within two working days" is one sentence and does a
disproportionate amount of work.

**8. Is anything on it untrue or out of date?**
Fees, availability, location, qualifications, membership, the list of
what you offer.

**9. Do you own it?**
Your domain in your own account, your content exportable, no monthly fee
you cannot stop without losing the site. If a website is rented, a
redesign is a good moment to stop renting.

**10. Does it sound like you?**
Read the homepage out loud. If you would not say those sentences to
somebody sitting opposite you, that is the thing to fix — and it is a
writing problem, not a design one.

---

## What to do with the answers

**Mostly yes, one or two nos.** Fix the nos. Do not redesign.

**Nos clustered around content — fees, who it is for, what happens
next.** The words are the problem. A redesign will help only if it comes
with a rethink of what the site says, which is a different and usually
more valuable piece of work than choosing a new look.

**Nos clustered around the practical — phone, contact, ownership, it
looks wrong on a phone.** That is a build problem, and it is the clearest
case for starting again. Websites of a certain age cannot be made
responsive by editing them.

**You cannot answer question one at all.** That is worth knowing. It is
not a website problem yet — it is a question about what the practice is,
and it is much cheaper to answer it before somebody builds you a site
around a guess.

---

## One more thing

Before you commission anything, write down what you want to be different
in six months' time. Not "a better website" — something you could
actually notice. Fewer enquiries from people you cannot help. Being
willing to give somebody the address. Not having to explain your fees in
every first email.

Whoever you work with, that sentence is the most useful thing you can
hand them.
