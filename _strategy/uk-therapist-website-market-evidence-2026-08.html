Session fees and practitioner income

No primary BACP or UKCP fee survey was located. The figures below come from convergent secondary sources and are Inferred, not verified:

  • Typical UK private counselling sessions £40–£70; specialists, psychologists and central London £65–£150+. One source states that "more than half of the therapists listed on [BACP's] directory advertise between £40 and £60" (Wobble, updated Aug 2026). Counselling Directory's own article gives trainees £15–£25, qualified £30–£60, specialists £60–£150+ (source).
  • No official survey of annual private-practice income was found. Blog-based worked examples range from roughly £30,500 take-home to £52,800 gross, explicitly not sourced to any survey. These should be treated as illustrations, not data.

04

Observed weaknesses in therapist websites

Two specialist critiques document named, observed defects rather than generic usability advice. Both were retrieved in full: Design for Therapists (12 items) and High Five Design (20 items). Verified

  • Specific defects observed on live sites

    An entirely empty footer. A "This website was created by Wix.com" watermark still present in the header. Verbatim-copied textbook passages describing therapy approaches. A Bitmoji cartoon used in place of a professional headshot.

  • Withheld information

    Fees and insurance details omitted. The source attributes this to therapists fearing that stated prices deter enquiries, and observes that omission "often creates more hesitation" instead. Specialisms buried inside long About-page paragraphs rather than stated up front. Contact and booking routes hidden, requiring visitors to "play detective."

  • Language

    Professional jargon — "modality," "somatic attunement," "evidence-based integrative approach" — used in place of the plain terms a prospective client would search for. About sections turning "memoir-like" with childhood pets and favourite holidays. Over-reliance on quotation: "your website should reflect you, not the Brené fan club."

  • Positioning

    Sites attempting to serve "individuals, couples, teens, families" and multiple presenting issues simultaneously. The source connects this directly to weak search visibility as well as unclear messaging.

  • Imagery

    "Generic stock photos of people staring out windows and holding mugs," lotuses and sunsets, and "chameleon" text blending into its background.

  • Technical and typographic debt

    Arial-era typefaces, double spacing after punctuation, type too small to read, and credential badges clustered in clashing colours and sizes.

  • Group practice navigation

    Visitors required to open every individual practitioner's bio page in turn rather than being given a single comparison view.

Observation on the pattern. Across both sources, the documented failures are predominantly informational and editorial — missing fees, buried specialisms, hidden contact routes, borrowed language — rather than matters of visual taste. This describes the composition of these two lists; it is not a general claim about the population of therapist websites, which was not sampled.

Notable examples

UK therapist sites identified as well-executed, with the specific features observed on each. Assessment of design quality is inherently a judgement; what is recorded here is what is verifiably present on each page.

  • Shonagh Wright-Phillips Counselling — Colchester, North Essex

    Muted green and dark-ink palette; naturalistic photography of fields and plants with candid portraiture; copy carrying a consistent growth metaphor ("like nature, growth can be gentle," "refugia"); enquiry calls to action placed consistently and prominently rather than buried.

  • Sally Blackmore Counselling — built by Kirsty M Design

    Earthy palette with leaf iconography; nature photography selected to signal safety and support; content deliberately broken into scannable sections rather than dense blocks. A directly comparable example of a UK Squarespace build at the £1,700+ band.

  • Dr Ramona Burrage

    Gold-accent, teal and neutral palette; copy naming specific modalities (CBT, ACT, EMDR) alongside a Kintsugi metaphor without lapsing into jargon; calls to action present but deliberately understated.

  • Therapy in London — a directory, not a single practice

    Press-logo trust bar (Guardian, Independent, GQ, Yahoo News); a numbered three-step process reducing the perceived complexity of finding a therapist; a distinctive envelope and stamp illustration motif.

  • International reference points

    From Wildberry Studio's curated list: The Practice (custom modality icons, a lime-green palette departing from the category norm); Everroot (restrained neutral minimalism); Maciej Maćkowiak (horizontal-scroll navigation built around a forest metaphor — the most structurally unusual example found).

06

Professional and advertising rules

Distinguishing legal requirement, professional-body rule, and guidance. The ASA/CAP Code has legal underpinning through the Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008 and is enforceable by Trading Standards; body rules bind members only.

BACP

The 2018 Ethical Framework contains no standalone advertising section. The operative clauses are general: §43, "We will maintain high standards of honesty and probity in all aspects of our work"; §45, "Whenever we communicate our qualifications, professional experience and working methods, we will do so accurately and honestly." Verified · Framework PDF

Designation wording is prescribed exactly. Only current paid members may use these terms, and only in these forms: "Registered Member of BACP" / "Registered counsellor" / "Registered Member MBACP"; "Accredited Member of BACP" / "Registered Member MBACP (Accred)"; "Registered Member MBACP (Snr Accred)". On lapse or cancellation a member "may not refer to yourself using any designatory letters or titles" — with no grace period stated. Verified · BACP

Logo use: "You can display your logo on your website and printed materials" but "only use the logos in their original format – don't change things like the colour, typeface, orientation or aspect ratio." Verified

UKCP — an explicit testimonial prohibition

Two UKCP documents were retrieved and agree. The Code of Ethics and Professional Practice 2019, clause 13: "Not make any claims which you cannot demonstrate to be true or include testimonials from clients in any advertising." Clause 11 requires advertising that is "accurate and in a responsible and professional manner, without exaggeration." Clause 12 governs titles and post-nominals, requiring that use of "Dr" indicate whether it is medical or academic. The organisational Ethical Principles and Code of Professional Conduct mirrors this at §11.1 and §11.3. Verified · UKCP 2019 Code

NCPS

The dedicated advertising-policy URL returned no retrievable policy text, and a Code of Ethics PDF found in search returned 404. A secondary source, not independently verified against NCPS primary text, reports that the Code requires advertising presenting "a truthful, honest and accurate picture" and directs members to CAP Code compliance rather than prohibiting testimonials. Not verified — see §10.

ASA and the CAP Code — applies to all advertisers

  • Rule 12.2: claims that a therapy can "diagnose, treat, help with or cure a medical condition" are unacceptable unless the advertiser is "suitably qualified to diagnose or treat that symptom or condition" and holds supporting evidence.
  • Rule 12.1: advertisers must hold "robust evidence to support the efficacy claim."
  • ASA hypnotherapy guidance, applicable by analogy: "Claims to treat depression, addiction, eating disorders and other serious mental or psychological conditions are likely to be considered claims to treat serious medical conditions and practitioners should not refer to the treatment of these conditions unless that treatment is to be carried out under the supervision of a suitably qualified health professional."
  • Testimonials, rules 3.48–3.49: factual claims within testimonials must not mislead, and — the operative point — "testimonials alone do not constitute substantiation so marketers should not rely on testimonials as support for any direct or implied claims."

Verified ASA health therapies · ASA hypnotherapy · ASA testimonials

Documented enforcement precedent

Stockport Counselling Services (therapist Dawn Hazeldine). The website stated she was a member of BACP and "was accredited by the body." The ASA found the claim could not be substantiated — she was registered, not accredited. The advertiser stated she "had intended to remove the claim but could not access the website to do so." Outcome: the advertisement "must not appear again in its current form." Advertisement seen September 2024; ruling published 12 March 2025. The complaint was brought by BACP itself.

Verified via trade reporting of the adjudication. The ASA's own ruling page was not retrieved directly — see §10.

CQC registration

CQC registration is required for "regulated activities" under the Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations, the relevant one being "treatment of disease, disorder or injury." Secondary sources consistently describe this as centred on medical and clinical treatment — diagnosis, prescribing, treatment under the Mental Health Act 1983 — rather than standalone talking therapy delivered by a counsellor or psychotherapist without a medical qualification. cqc.org.uk blocked automated retrieval on every attempt, so no primary quotation was obtained. Not verified — see §10.

07

Data protection

Special category data in enquiry forms

ICO guidance states that "Article 9 prohibits the processing of special category data" subject to conditions. Health data is special category. For a therapist the relevant condition is "(h) Health or social care (with a basis in law)", and DPA 2018 §11(1) "clarifies when the requirement for a professional obligation of secrecy will be met under UK law."

Two separate tests must both be satisfied: an Article 6 lawful basis and an Article 9 condition plus a DPA 2018 Schedule 1 condition. Verified · ICO

Observation. A contact form inviting a visitor to describe what brings them to therapy will collect health data as soon as anyone answers substantively. This is a statement about what Article 9 covers, not advice on form design.

Controller and processor status

ICO's test: "If you exercise overall control of the purpose and means of the processing of personal data – ie, you decide what data to process and why – you are a controller. If you don't have any purpose of your own for processing the data and you only act on a client's instructions, you are likely to be a processor." On that test, a studio that builds a site and hands it over, without hosting or processing enquiries afterwards, is typically neither; the therapist is sole controller. A studio whose own tooling handles enquiry data on an ongoing basis would fall into processor status with its own obligations. Verified · ICO

Cookies and PECR — mid-revision

  • Established position: consent is required for first-party analytics cookies, and a consent mechanism that "emphasizes 'agree' or 'allow' over 'reject' or 'block' represents a non-compliant approach."
  • Change in progress: the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, enacted 19 June 2025, amends PECR to introduce five purpose-limited exemptions where consent will not be required, with an opt-out right retained. ICO issued updated draft cookies guidance in July 2025; a second consultation closed 26 September 2025; final guidance was expected in 2026. PECR penalties are being aligned upward toward UK GDPR levels.
  • Enforcement activity: ICO reviewed the top 1,000 UK websites for cookie compliance from January 2025. Of 200 assessed, 134 received warning letters — for pre-ticked boxes, non-prominent reject buttons and lack of granular consent. The sweep targeted the largest UK sites, not small businesses.

Verified Burges Salmon on the DUAA changes · ICO review reporting. Whether the final ICO guidance has since been published was not confirmed — see §10.

08

Accessibility law

  • Equality Act 2010 — a duty, but not a WCAG mandate

    Section 20 places an anticipatory reasonable-adjustments duty on service providers — not a reactive duty triggered by complaint. The three limbs are modifying practices that disadvantage disabled people, removing or altering barriers, and providing auxiliary aids. Adjustments need only be "reasonable", weighed against organisation size, cost and practicability. WCAG 2.0, 2.1 and 2.2 are not themselves legally mandated for private-sector businesses under current UK law, though WCAG is the standard a regulator or court would be likely to reference. Verified via Taylor Wessing analysis

  • No UK private-sector case law located

    No UK legal case establishing precedent against a private business specifically for an inaccessible website was found, in contrast to extensive US ADA litigation. The same source describes UK enforcement as "patchy at best," attributing this to the absence of a dedicated regulator and the cost of court action. Absence of evidence — recorded in §10.

  • WCAG 2.2 status

    Became a W3C Recommendation on 12 December 2024. WCAG 3.0 is in parallel development, described by W3C as "a more substantial restructuring... a multi-year effort" — not a near-term replacement. Verified · W3C

  • European Accessibility Act

    In force 28 June 2025, with pre-existing services having until 28 June 2030. It does not transpose into UK law, but binds UK businesses supplying in-scope goods and services to EU consumers. Annex I scope is a closed list: computers and operating systems, ATMs and ticketing machines, smartphones, TV equipment, telephony, audiovisual media access, passenger transport, banking, e-books and e-commerce. Micro-enterprises are exempt; the numerical threshold was not confirmed from primary EU text. A UK therapist's brochure site with no EU clients and no e-commerce falls outside the listed sectors. Verified · AbilityNet

09

Search visibility and how clients choose

Google's own stated position

  • E-E-A-T and YMYL. Google's developer documentation: "experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness, or what we call E-E-A-T", and "our systems give even more weight to content that aligns with strong E-E-A-T for topics that could significantly impact the health, financial stability, or safety of people." Mental health falls within that category. "Of these aspects, trust is most important." On authorship, Google "strongly encourage[s] adding accurate authorship information, such as bylines", ideally leading to further information about the author. Verified · Google
  • Local ranking. Google Business Profile Help states local results are ranked on "Relevance, distance, and popularity/prominence", with prominence based on "website links, reviews, and ratings" among other signals. Service-area-business specifics for a home-based practitioner were not covered in the pages retrieved. Verified · Google
  • Core Web Vitals. Google states "Core Web Vitals are used by our ranking systems" but also that "Google Search always seeks to show the most relevant content, even if the page experience is sub-par", and that page experience contributes "for many queries [where] there is lots of helpful content available." Current thresholds for a "good" rating: LCP ≤ 2.5s, INP ≤ 200ms, CLS ≤ 0.1. Verified · Google · web.dev

Research on how clients select a therapist

UK doctoral research, directly on point

May, J. (2018). What factors influence a client's choice of counsellor or psychotherapist in a private practice setting? Doctoral thesis, University of Brighton (supervisor: Diane Waller).

Findings: third-party recommendation was the primary selection factor. Where unavailable, clients searched online for location, experience and perceived relatability. Clients "assessed these factors using the photograph and rhetoric on the therapist's website or directory entry, as well as during the first face-to-face meeting," relying substantially on "unconscious projection and 'gut instinct'." Qualifications, professional standing and modality were given comparatively little weight. The study also found widespread public misassumption that the profession is statutorily regulated, and low client awareness of the need to verify credentials.

Verified · University of Brighton repository. Single qualitative study; sample size and composition not extracted — see §10.

  • Facial attractiveness and therapist selection

    "Facial attractiveness increases the likelihood of choosing a psychotherapist, irrespective of sexual preferences and perceived trustworthiness." Psychotherapy Research, 2025, DOI 10.1080/10503307.2025.2494272, PubMed 40356087. Title, journal and DOI verified via PubMed indexing; full text was blocked by the publisher, so no figures, method or sample details were extracted. Partially verified

  • First-impression formation

    Lindgaard, G., Fernandes, G., Dudek, C., & Brown, J. (2006). Attention web designers: You have 50 milliseconds to make a good first impression! Behaviour & Information Technology, 25(2), 115–126. Citation confirmed across the publisher record, Semantic Scholar and ResearchGate; 1,000+ citations. Full text was returned as 403, so the headline finding — visual-appeal judgements forming within roughly 50ms and correlating with later credibility judgements — is reported from the established literature rather than re-extracted. Citation verified, text not retrieved

  • Web credibility factors

    Stanford Web Credibility Project guidelines: verifiable information and citations, visible legitimacy markers such as a physical address, clear display of credentials and professional affiliations, humanising about-content, easy contact details, polished consistent visual design, genuine usability, visible freshness, minimal overt advertising, and error-free presentation. Published 2002 — foundational and widely cited, but not current empirical data. Verified · Stanford

10

Research limitations

Evidence that does not exist publicly

  1. What proportion of UK therapists have their own website. Searched extensively across industry blogs, professional bodies and marketing agencies serving this sector. No published figure of any kind was located. This is a genuine gap in the literature, not merely an unverified number.
  2. Where UK therapists actually get clients from. No credible survey quantifying the split between Google organic and local search, Counselling Directory, Psychology Today and word-of-mouth referral was found. Counselling Directory's own statistics page gives only a supply-side figure. Any channel-mix claim from any source should be treated as anecdote.
  3. Official session-fee and income data. No primary BACP or UKCP fee survey exists. The £40–£70 and £65–£150 bands in §1 are secondary syntheses of directory listings, and the income figures are blog worked examples explicitly not sourced to any survey.

Sources that blocked retrieval

  1. CQC (cqc.org.uk) returned 403 or 404 on every attempt. The registration boundary in §6 rests on secondary sources with no primary quotation. Confirm via CQC's "Scope of registration" guidance or a direct enquiry before relying on it.
  2. NCPS advertising policy. The dedicated policy URL returned no content; a Code of Ethics PDF found in search returned 404. NCPS's position on testimonials is characterised from a secondary source only and should not be assumed to mirror UKCP's prohibition.
  3. The ASA ruling itself. The Stockport Counselling Services adjudication is reported via trade press, not read from the ASA's own database. The primary ruling should be located on asa.org.uk before the case is cited anywhere public.
  4. Psychotherapy Research 2025 paper. Publisher bot-check blocked the full text. Title, journal and DOI are confirmed; effect sizes, method and sample are not.
  5. Lindgaard et al. (2006). Returned 403. Citation confirmed; text not re-read.
  6. The Digital Therapist could not be retrieved, so is absent from the price comparison in §3.

Figures needing a direct check

  1. WordPress.com and Webflow prices are in USD. The pages did not render GBP to this retrieval. Check both from a UK connection before quoting sterling figures.
  2. Squarespace and Wix GBP figures are from third-party UK pricing guides, not the platforms' own pages. Verify against squarespace.com and wix.com directly.
  3. ICO cookie guidance was mid-consultation when the sources in §7 were written, with final guidance expected in 2026. Whether it has since been published was not confirmed.
  4. EAA micro-enterprise threshold. The exemption is confirmed to exist; the numerical staff and turnover threshold was not confirmed from primary EU text.
  5. BABCP, COSCA, ACC and BPS current membership. No current total was found for any of these. The BPS figure circulating (60,604) dates from 2019. HCPC does not publish counselling psychologists separately from the 32,206 all-specialism total.

Methodological caveats

  1. The PCPB aggregate contains double-counting. Practitioners holding dual BACP and UKCP registration are counted twice; the overlap is known to be material and is not quantified anywhere. No de-duplicated national figure for unique practitioners exists.
  2. The provider list in §3 is a search sample, not a census. Sixteen UK providers were found; there may be others, particularly sole traders without search visibility. Statements about price clustering and gaps describe this sample only.
  3. "No UK therapist template shop was found" is absence of evidence. It reflects the searches run, not a proven absence.
  4. The weaknesses in §4 come from two curated critique articles written by competing designers, who have a commercial interest in the failures they document. No systematic sample of therapist websites was taken, and the list should not be read as representative of the population.
  5. The examples in §5 involve aesthetic judgement. What is recorded is the features observed on each page; "well-executed" is not a measured property.
  6. WebSearch is US-weighted. UK-specific results were reached largely through site-targeted queries and direct retrieval, which may have missed UK providers or sources that rank poorly in a US-biased index.
  7. All prices and page contents are as at 22 August 2026 and will drift. Anything load-bearing should be re-checked at the point of use.

Outside the competence of this document

  1. Nothing here is legal advice. The Article 9 analysis, the consumer-contract position, licensing wording and the CQC boundary all require a UK solicitor. The professional-body rules are quoted accurately but their application to a specific practitioner's circumstances is not something desk research can settle.
