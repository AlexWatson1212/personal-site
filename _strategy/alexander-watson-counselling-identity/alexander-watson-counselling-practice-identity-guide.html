A practice written down before a website is designed for it.

The website is one expression of this practice. This guide is the practice: what it stands for, who it is written for, how it sounds and what it looks like. Whatever gets made next – a leaflet, a directory profile, a sign, a second site – starts from here rather than from the last thing made.

Three kinds of statement

Established Something you told us. Your training, your placement, your themes, your supplied logos.

Interpretation Something derived from what you told us. The central idea, the reader, the voice. You are entitled to say it is wrong.

Recommendation A creative or strategic decision. Colour, type, structure. Each has a reason; if the reason is wrong, so is the decision.

Each section opens with a line saying which of the three it mostly is.

What this document is not

It is not public copy, and Part One and pages A–D are never sent to a supplier. They contain more about you than a client should ever read, because the identity had to be derived from you. For every personal fact the test was: does knowing this help a prospective client understand what it would be like to work with Alexander? It is not a claim about your competence. Nothing in it describes what you are qualified to do.

The central idea
1Nothing repeats for no reason.
2Nothing repeats for no reason.
3Nothing repeats for no reason.

What keeps happening in a life is usually doing something – avoiding a failure or a success, keeping someone at a workable distance, saving energy that isn’t there. That is why understanding it so often doesn’t change it: the explanation arrives, and the job it was doing still needs doing. The practice exists to find out, without a verdict and at the person’s pace, what it is doing and what it costs. What they do about it stays theirs. And the reason is usually more ordinary than the story told about it.

Basis: interpretation of your twelve themes · the organising idea · said in public once, in the three hundred words

#Your themeHow the idea holds it
01Accuracy before reassuranceAn accurate account of what something is doing. Sometimes the accurate account is that the situation is hard and nothing needs decoding.
02Noticing rather than fixingThe reason is found by noticing, and handed back. The idea never tells the counsellor to remove the repeat.
03Understanding is not changeIts central claim: knowing the story isn’t the same as the job being finished.
04Success and failureNot acting repeats because it protects from both outcomes at once.
05CapacitySome repeats have the plainest reason of all: there isn’t enough in the tank. Reading that as character is the mistake.
06ShameThe shame loop is a repeat with a reason – shame makes the task heavier, which feeds the avoidance.
07Masking and performanceThe acceptable version keeps being produced because it has kept something safe. It also costs.
08AutonomyA reason found together is not a verdict delivered. The person decides what, if anything, changes.
09RelationshipsCloseness and distance repeat across relationships. The stories told about other people repeat too.
10ContradictionTwo real needs are often the reason. The repeat serves one of them at the other’s expense.
11Not a problem to solveA repeat with a reason is a person making sense, not a person who is broken.
12DirectnessIt is five plain words. It needs no vocabulary to be understood.
Where it comes from in your training

Psychodynamic thinking has long held that what isn’t understood tends to be repeated, and that it shows up again in the relationship with the counsellor. Person-centred thinking locates much distress in the gap between experience and the “shoulds” a person has absorbed. The idea is both, said in plain English. That matters for the five-year test: it will still be true after you qualify.

Its limit, written in from the start

Not everything is a pattern. Caring for someone, losing someone, a relationship ending: sometimes the accurate response is yes, that is hard, and the work is staying with it. The issues you describe from your placement – bereavement, responsibility, changed circumstances – are often exactly this. A practice that decoded grief as a pattern would be using its idea as a hammer.

02Professional identity

More interested in what something is doing than in what it says about you.

Six characteristics that hold across everything the practice does and says. They are descriptions of how you already think and work, written down so they can be repeated and checked.

Basis: interpretation of your stated themes

Asks what it is doing

Before a behaviour is treated as a flaw, it is treated as information. The first question about anything that keeps happening is what it is for, and the second is what it costs.

Says the plain true thing

Including that is hard and I don’t know yet. Accuracy is offered as a form of respect. It is never used as permission to be harsh.

Notices, then hands it back

Observations are offered to be checked, and withdrawn if they are wrong. What the person does with any of it stays theirs. Nobody’s life is redesigned in the room.

Keeps two things in view

Wanting closeness and wanting to be left alone; loving someone and resenting what it asks. Both can be real, and neither has to win early. Contradiction is usually where the work starts.

Reads capacity before character

A task that isn’t getting done is first a question about what is left in the tank. I should be able to do this is examined, not obeyed.

Notices what is missing

The gap in a story, the difference between how something is presented and how it is lived. Your design work trained this habit. It shapes the attention and is mentioned once, plainly, if at all.

What the characteristics leave out, on purpose

Warm, empathic, non-judgemental, compassionate. They are all things a counsellor should be, and all things every counsellor says. Written as adjectives they are claims. The characteristics above describe behaviour that a client can check, which is where warmth actually shows.

How your other work is used

Your design and writing background explains the attention to language, contradiction and presentation. It is context. It does not appear as a selling point, a visual theme or a metaphor for therapy, and the counselling practice never shows design work.

03Philosophy of practice

Knowing why isn’t the same as being free of it.

This is the belief the practice runs on. If it is wrong, most of what follows changes – the reader, the position, the voice and the visual idea were all derived from it.

Basis: interpretation · to be checked against your own view before anything else

  1. Things repeat because they work – at something. Not acting protects against failing and against succeeding. Keeping a distance protects against being left. The acceptable version of yourself protects a place in the group. Each of these has a cost, which is usually why the person has come.
  2. Insight is necessary and rarely enough. A person can understand their history, their attachments and their fears in detail and carry on as before, because the explanation doesn’t do the job the repeat was doing. That gap is not a failure of intelligence or effort.
  3. Capacity is not character. Reading limited capacity as a moral failure creates shame, and shame makes the next task heavier. Decisions made while depleted are rarely the ones a person would make otherwise.
  4. Two needs can both be real. The work doesn’t require choosing between them early, and the contradiction is not a symptom.
  5. The person is the authority on their own life. The counsellor’s job is to notice and say, and to be corrected. The person decides what, if anything, to do.
  6. Not everything is a pattern. Some things are simply hard, and the accurate response is to say so and stay with it.
Where it sits in your training

The first and second beliefs are psychodynamic in origin; the third and fifth are person-centred. None of them needs its vocabulary to be understood, and the practice never uses the vocabulary to sound serious.

What it deliberately doesn’t believe

That there is a true self waiting under the performed one. That a person arrives broken. That understanding guarantees change, or that change always means doing less of something.

11Voice – the principles

Plain, dry and attentive. An adult talking to an adult across a table.

Seven principles. They describe how you already speak when you are being careful, with the casualness taken down one notch and nothing else removed.

Basis: interpretation of your stated communication style

01 · Say the ordinary version

“It keeps happening”, not “recurring relational patterns”. If a clinical or theoretical word is needed, it is used once and explained in the same sentence.

02 · Describe behaviour, never feelings

Situations and actions a reader can recognise. The practice never names a feeling on someone’s behalf – not in a headline, not in a caption, not in a reply.

03 · Allow the true, uncomfortable sentence

“That is hard.” “I don’t know yet.” “I might be wrong about this.” Short, unsoftened, and not followed by a silver lining.

04 · Hand it back

Where a piece reaches a decision, the decision is the reader’s. Sentences end on their choice: “That’s your call.” “Whenever suits.”

05 · Keep both

Where two things are true, join them with “and”. “It makes sense, and it costs you.” Contradictions are stated, not resolved on the reader’s behalf.

06 · Short, then shorter

One idea to a sentence; three sentences to a paragraph at most. Readable by someone with very little left at the end of a day. This is the voice’s version of the design principle.

07 · Dry, never jokey

Understatement is allowed, and so is a plain observation that happens to be funny. No puns, no wordplay in headings, no exclamation marks, and never humour at a reader’s expense or about anything a client brings.

The sentence most likely to undo it

Reassurance in advance. Telling a reader it will be all right, that they are doing brilliantly or that they are brave – before anything is known about them – is the practice’s single most damaging sentence, because it is the one this reader has learned to distrust.

15Writing something new

A method for the things this guide couldn’t predict.

Sooner or later something will need writing that this guide didn’t foresee: a talk, a letter to another professional, a page about a change in how you work. This method keeps it in the same voice.

Basis: recommendation

  1. Write what you would say to one person, out loud. Don’t arrange it yet. The material is usually already there.
  2. Find the fact the reader needs first, and put it first. What it is, who it’s for, what it costs, when.
  3. Remove every feeling you assigned to the reader. Replace each with a situation or a behaviour, or delete it.
  4. Find the verdict hiding in the draft – a “should”, a “struggle”, a “finally” – and take it out.
  5. Name the limit. What this new thing isn’t, can’t do, or doesn’t know yet. Full size, first person.
  6. End on the reader’s decision. Then cut a third.
Then four tests

The retell test. Could a reader tell a friend what you work on, what you won’t claim and what it costs?

The swap test. Put another counsellor’s name on it. If nothing breaks, it isn’t yours yet.

The verdict test. Does any sentence decide what the reader is, or what their behaviour says about them?

The bad-day test. Could someone with very little left read it once and know what to do next?

The one movement

39Rules for future changes

How to change something without losing the rest.

The practice will change, and an identity that can’t change with it becomes something to work around. What matters is that each change is written down, so this guide keeps describing what exists.

Basis: recommendation

Four questions before adding anything
  1. What job does it do? One sentence. If the sentence is about how it looks, leave it out.
  2. Can something existing do that job? Usually: more space, a hairline, the plain answers, the count.
  3. Where exactly is it allowed? One named place. Something allowed everywhere becomes a second identity within a year.
  4. Is it written down? Below, and in the asset register.

    People who can see what keeps happening and for whom seeing hasn’t been enough, whatever their vocabulary – alongside people carrying something that is simply hard. Your supplied reader is kept as one of three, not as the definition.
